## Supporting quote

On the public already generating hazard information through the channels they have, and on what a formal system adds:

> "Crowdsourcing, a model to leverage crowd intelligence through online human input to serve specific organizational goals [10], mostly relies on gathering unstructured crowd intelligence through social media and online social networks. However, crowdsensing refers to a more structured type of data generation by crowds through physical sensors [11]."

On formal systems building on rather than replacing that behaviour:

> "Additionally, this work seeks to unveil the frameworks or models that can potentially guide disaster management authorities towards integrating crowd-sensed data with their existing decision-support systems."

On the specific reporting pattern Antas implements:

> "During the review process, it was observed that the crowd as reporters phenomenon, where users report on a situation using the sensing capabilities of smartphones and the report gets verified by an official, appears as a frequently used sensing solution. This idea adopts the concept of smart citizens for smart cities crowdsensing [50]. Smart citizens, or crowd as reporters as stated in this paper, are believed to be major drivers of smart cities and they have been increasingly active in sensed data generation through smart city applications [50]."

## How it supports the paper
The review states plainly that crowd contribution during disasters already happens as *unstructured* intelligence flowing through social media and online social networks, and positions the structured alternative — location- and sensor-anchored reporting from ordinary smartphones — as a refinement of that same behaviour, not a substitute for it. This is precisely Antas's premise: Metro Manila residents are already posting flood photos to Facebook groups and Messenger threads, and the system's contribution is structure, location and time rather than the impulse to share. The "crowd as reporters" pattern the review identifies as one of its most frequently recurring designs — a resident reports a situation from a smartphone, and an official verifies the report — is a direct description of Antas's five-level, GPS-pinned, timestamped reporting flow. The stated aim of guiding "disaster management authorities towards integrating crowd-sensed data with their existing decision-support systems" supports the claim that formal systems succeed by structuring and absorbing citizen reporting rather than displacing it.

## Caveats
- *Sensors* is an MDPI journal. It is Scopus/Web-of-Science indexed and legitimate, but if your marker is known to discount MDPI, swap in the Nielsen et al. (2024) alternate above — it is fully verified and ready to use.
- The Sensors review deliberately **excludes** studies scoped solely to social media ("crowdsourcing is not included if it is solely scoped to social media since there are open issues related to fake news and the reliability of such collected data"). This does not undercut the claim you are making — it is in fact the review drawing the same unstructured/structured distinction Antas draws — but do not cite it as evidence *about* social media data quality, only as evidence about the unstructured-to-structured shift.
- Verbatim text was extracted from the publisher-deposited XML rather than the typeset PDF, so whitespace around reference callouts was normalised. Wording is unaltered.
- No paywall issues: both the recommended source and the Nielsen alternate are open access.
