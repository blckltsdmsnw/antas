## Supporting quote
> "Although individuals rely on social media platforms for timely updates during crises, these platforms also facilitate the rapid spread of unverified and misleading information. Consequently, misinformation can hamper emergency response efforts, misdirect resources, and distort public perception of the disaster's true severity."

> "The selected studies revealed consistent patterns in how misinformation spreads during natural disasters, highlighting the role of users, some influencers, and bots in amplified false narratives. The misleading messages disseminated across social media platforms often outpaced official communications, resulting in reduced trust and exacerbating anxiety, stress, and fear among affected populations."

(Both passages are from the article Abstract. The second sentence recurs verbatim in the Results section. A parallel statement appears in the Discussion: "Delayed responses, by contrast, allow false narratives to proliferate, compounding the damage".)

## How it supports the paper
The review synthesises nine primary studies of misinformation during natural hazard events and reports the asymmetry Antas designs around: false and misleading claims propagate rapidly through the same channels residents use for "timely updates," while official communications — the corrective half — arrive behind them. That asymmetry is the argument against free-text comments beneath a depth reading: a comment field is exactly the unverified, unmoderated channel the review describes, and with nobody moderating in real time, a wrong or stale remark would outlive the conditions that produced it while any correction arrived late or not at all. It is also the argument against any "safe" label, since the review ties reduced trust and inappropriate protective action directly to reassuring content that official messaging failed to overtake in time.

## Caveats
- This is a narrative review, not a primary empirical study. It states the spread/correction asymmetry as a synthesised finding across nine included studies rather than measuring it directly. If the marker wants a primary source for the diffusion asymmetry, chase the Zhai et al. (2024) alternate above through library access.
- The review's literature search had no date restriction, so some of the underlying primary studies it summarises are older than 2022; the review itself is 2025 and its stated finding is the citable claim.
- The review covers natural disasters broadly (hurricanes, floods, earthquakes, wildfires) rather than urban flooding in Metro Manila specifically. Nothing Philippines-specific.
- It speaks of misinformation outpacing "official communications." That is slightly broader than "corrections" narrowly defined, though the Discussion's "Delayed responses, by contrast, allow false narratives to proliferate" narrows it usefully.
- Open access (CC BY), no paywall.
